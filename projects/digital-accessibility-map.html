Digital Accessibility Map

A public map of how accessible Brgy. Real in Calamba City is for people who use wheelchairs, walkers or crutches. Anyone can check a ramp, restroom or crossing before they leave home, and editors with their own accounts keep it up to date from the field.

What it looks like

What it does

Pins for places, lines for streets

Buildings, ramps and bus stops are pins. Streets, sidewalks and crossings are drawn as lines along the route. There are 14 categories, from schools and healthcare facilities to accessible restrooms.

A checklist for each place

A building can have a ramp, entrance, restroom, curb ramp, crossing, signage and PWD parking. Each one gets its own status and a short note, like "slope looks too steep".

Filters that match the feature

Search by name, or filter by status, category and feature. Filtering for a PWD ramp that exists still shows a place with a good ramp, even when the building as a whole needs work.

Map or list

Some people would rather read than pan a map, so every place is also in a list that sits beside the map on desktop and slides up on mobile.

Built for fieldwork

Editors log in, tap "Use my current location", add features and notes, and attach photos. Photos shrink automatically, carry a caption and are grouped by feature in the place's gallery.

Your data, your tools

One click downloads everything as CSV for Excel and Google Sheets, or as GeoJSON for QGIS and Google My Maps. The CSV export neutralises spreadsheet formulas in typed text.

Keyboard and screen reader friendly

Designed mobile-first and for keyboard and screen-reader use, aiming at WCAG 2.1 AA. Status never relies on colour alone.

Reading the map

Four statuses, each with its own icon and shape as well as a colour.

  • ✓Existing. The feature is there and usable. Shown as a green circle.
  • !Needs improvement. It's there but has barriers, like a steep ramp or a blocked sidewalk. Shown in orange.
  • +Proposed. Not built yet, but recommended. Shown as a blue square.
  • ?Needs further assessment. Not enough information yet. Shown in grey.

How it's built

A Next.js app with the App Router and TypeScript, styled with Tailwind. The map uses Leaflet and OpenStreetMap, which need no API key. Data lives in Neon Postgres, written as plain SQL with no ORM, and photos go to Vercel Blob. It runs on Vercel's free tier.

  • Next.js
  • TypeScript

  • Vercel

Anyone can read the map, but only editors can change it. Every write endpoint checks the session, passwords are hashed, sessions use a signed httpOnly cookie, and no secrets are committed to the repository.

Where it stands

The map holds 22 places. Their details come from a desk assessment of OpenStreetMap data and public pages, and each one is marked "confirm on site". The next step is fieldwork. A guide in the repository, in English and Filipino, shows student volunteers (NSTP) what to record at each stop so it goes straight into the map.
